# Methodology Part 1 — Experience Organization

## Purpose

This part of the methodology is used to reconstruct personal experience before writing a resume, personal statement, portfolio, or research application.

The goal is **not** to make the experience sound impressive at the beginning.

The goal is to preserve what actually happened, how the person acted, and what the experience meant to them.

---

# Core Idea

Use the **STAR-T principle** as the basic framework. It extends STAR with one final **T for Takeaway**.

The user speaks or types the experience in their own natural language.

AI then organizes the raw description into a structured experience record.

The output at this stage is:

> **raw but structured experience documentation**

It is not yet resume language.

---

# Step 1 — Raw Self-Narration

The user recalls one experience at a time.

The experience can be described through:
- typing;
- speech-to-text;
- voice conversation;
- rough notes.

The user should **not** try to sound professional.

The user should speak naturally and include:
- context;
- technical details;
- decisions;
- mistakes;
- feelings;
- team problems;
- failures;
- things they enjoyed;
- things they disliked;
- what they learned;
- what changed afterward.

The priority is:

> **remember first, polish later**

---

# Step 2 — Use STAR-T as the Structural Skeleton

For each experience, organize the raw content into:

## S — Situation
What was happening?

Questions:
- What was the project, contest, course, internship, or event?
- When did it happen?
- Who was involved?
- What was the context?
- Why did the user enter this experience?

## T — Task
What was the user responsible for?

Questions:
- What problem needed to be solved?
- What was expected from the user?
- What role did the user have?
- What was the goal?

## A — Action
What did the user actually do?

Questions:
- What decisions did the user make?
- What did the user build, design, analyze, organize, or implement?
- What tools, methods, or technologies were used?
- What problems were debugged?
- What part did the user personally own?

## R — Result
What happened in the end?

Questions:
- Was the project completed?
- Was there an award, publication, deployment, score, or working system?
- Did the project fail?
- What was the final outcome?

---

# Step 3 — Add Reflection

STAR alone is not enough for long-term experience organization.

Add the following fields:

## Personal Takeaway
What did this experience mean personally?

Examples:
- first time leading a team;
- first major failure;
- first time building a complete system;
- first exposure to research;
- first time realizing a field was not a good fit.

## What I Learned
What knowledge, skills, or working methods were gained?

Examples:
- technical tools;
- research workflow;
- system design;
- team coordination;
- debugging;
- task decomposition.

## What I Liked
What parts of the work gave energy or satisfaction?

This is important for later discovering genuine interests.

Examples:
- system integration;
- hardware debugging;
- mathematical decomposition;
- writing;
- working with a strong team;
- building a physical system.

## What I Disliked
What parts felt frustrating, boring, inefficient, or misaligned?

This is equally important.

Examples:
- remote collaboration;
- weak teammates;
- pure modeling;
- repetitive software work;
- final polishing;
- lack of ownership.

## Anything Else I Remember
Capture details that do not fit elsewhere.

This can include:
- emotional reactions;
- specific failures;
- team dynamics;
- unexpected events;
- technical details;
- ideas that may matter later.

---

# Step 4 — AI's Role

AI should act as an **organizer**, not an author.

AI should:
- preserve the user's facts;
- sort content into the correct fields;
- remove unnecessary repetition;
- make chronology readable;
- keep technical details;
- retain failures and negative experiences;
- preserve the user's subjective interpretation;
- mark uncertain facts for later verification;
- avoid inventing meaning that the user did not express.

AI should **not**:
- rewrite the experience immediately into resume bullets;
- make the experience sound more impressive than it was;
- force a research narrative too early;
- delete inconvenient details;
- infer motivation without evidence;
- replace the user's interpretation with generic career advice.

---

# Step 5 — Do Not Summarize Too Early

A critical rule:

> **Do not let AI summarize before the user finishes remembering.**

Important details often appear late in the narration.

For example, the user may first describe:
- the project;
- the technical task;
- the result;

and only later remember:
- why they disliked the project;
- what they learned about teamwork;
- why the experience changed their direction;
- what kind of work they actually enjoy.

Premature summarization can remove the most valuable information.

---

# Step 6 — Preserve Raw and Reconstructed Versions Separately

For important experiences or higher-level reflections, keep two versions:

## Raw Version
The user's original wording and thoughts.

Purpose:
- preserve the original meaning;
- keep the source of truth;
- avoid losing nuance.

## Reconstructed Version
A cleaner, structured interpretation.

Purpose:
- make the experience easier to understand;
- reveal patterns;
- prepare for later resume or personal-statement work.

The reconstructed version should never replace the raw version.

---

# Step 7 — Store Experiences Chronologically

Maintain an experience archive:

- Experience 01
- Experience 02
- Experience 03
- ...

Each experience should contain the same structure.

This makes it possible later to analyze:
- how interests changed;
- how technical depth increased;
- how leadership evolved;
- how failures changed behavior;
- which kinds of work repeatedly gave energy;
- which skills transferred across fields.

---

# Standard Experience Record Template

```text
## Experience XX — Title

### Time
Approximate or exact date.

### Situation
What was happening?

### Task
What was I responsible for?

### Action
What did I actually do?

### Result
What happened?

### Personal Takeaway
What did this experience mean to me?

### What I Learned
What did I learn?

### What I Liked
What gave me energy?

### What I Disliked
What did not fit me?

### Anything Else I Remember
Additional details.

### Details to Verify Later
Dates, names, numbers, technical specifications, etc.
```

---

# Output of Part 1

The output should be a **chronological experience archive**.

This archive is the source material for later stages such as:
- personal trajectory reconstruction;
- identifying recurring strengths;
- identifying real interests;
- finding repeated working patterns;
- defining "What I Can Provide";
- defining "What I'm Looking For";
- selecting resume experiences;
- writing personal statements;
- writing professor outreach emails;
- building a personal website.

---

# One-Sentence Summary

> **Experience Organization = Raw self-narration + STAR-T structure + reflection fields + AI-assisted organization, while preserving the user's original meaning and delaying polish until later.**

---

# Core Principle

> **Remember first. Structure second. Interpret third. Polish last.**
